About

About me.

I’m Zhengning Xu, a Computer Science and Technology undergraduate at ShanghaiTech University. I work across AI for Biology and generative world models, guided by a question about what makes an AI result scientifically meaningful.

Curiosity across disciplines.

My research interests center on AI for Science: how to formulate a scientific question so that a model can help answer it. I am especially interested in computational biology, multimodal learning, and generative world models.

My experience has moved across genetic interactions, wearable motion sensing, mouse behavior, cancer drug response, and video generation. Each setting forced me to think about a different part of the same problem: what has to be measured or represented, what should the model be asked to do, and what evidence would make its output credible?

I enjoy the point where machine learning meets a domain question that existing benchmarks do not quite capture. That is where an apparently technical modeling task often becomes a question about scientific judgment.

Research philosophy

Choose the question before optimizing the answer.

Modern AI is becoming remarkably good at optimization. Once a task is reduced to a dataset, a metric, and an objective, much of the remaining loop—implementation, debugging, experimentation, ablation, and iterative improvement—can increasingly be accelerated by coding agents. This changes what I find most intellectually interesting about research.

The harder question is often no longer simply how to optimize an objective, but whether the objective deserves to be optimized at all.

A drug-response model may achieve lower error while offering explanations that cannot be validated. A behavior-recognition system may achieve high accuracy while operating in the wrong representational space. A video generator may produce visually convincing futures while failing to model the causal process that produced them.

These are not edge cases. To me, they are often the real research problem.

I therefore work from the scientific claim backward. I start by asking what capability the system is supposed to possess, what evidence would support that claim, and what failure mode current benchmarks may be hiding. Only then do I decide whether the solution requires a new model, a new representation, a better benchmark, a causal intervention, or a different validation framework.

This is how I think about AI for Science: formulating scientific questions into computational problems that are actually worth optimizing.

How my questions developed

What each project taught me.

Different domains have sharpened the same research instinct.

01 / Measurement

Start with what the data can express.

Working on a fabric wristband showed me why gesture recognition depends on how a motion is represented. I used Dynamic Time Warping to compare swings despite differences in timing. In MouseGPT, I encountered a larger version of the question: should animal behavior be restricted to predefined labels, or can language provide a richer space for discovery?

02 / Context

Ask where a prediction holds.

In synthetic lethality, the same gene pair may behave differently across cancer cell lines. That project made biological context a first-class modeling question for me. A model's output is only as meaningful as the cellular setting it actually represents.

03 / Evidence

Examine explanations systematically.

In drug-response modeling, I became interested in the limits of accuracy alone. HappyRabbit combines a controlled comparison of prediction models with attribution analysis, in-silico perturbations, pathway analysis, and literature auditing to ask whether explanations hold up beyond selected examples.

04 / Causality

Test what a generative model understands.

CausalCascade carries the same instinct into video generation. A model can produce a convincing sequence while missing the dependencies that make events unfold. Structured event graphs and controlled interventions let us ask whether it has modeled a process rather than only its appearance.

Current research

Two directions, one standard of evidence.

AI for Biology

Make biological predictions meaningful in context.

I am interested in AI systems that model biological systems while preserving the context, heterogeneity, and structure that make biological predictions meaningful. My work ranges from context-specific synthetic lethality and drug-response prediction to multimodal animal-behavior understanding.

Across these projects, I am particularly interested in moving beyond raw prediction toward representations and explanations that can support intervention, mechanism, and discovery. In synthetic lethality, this means taking seriously the fact that genetic interactions change across cellular environments. In drug response, it means testing explanations systematically under a consistent benchmark.

Generative world models

Find out when generation becomes modeling.

I am interested in whether generative models can move from producing plausible observations to representing how the world actually evolves. A visually convincing future is not necessarily a physically or causally correct one.

A useful world model should preserve entities and states, respect event dependencies, and respond selectively to interventions. My work on CausalCascade studies this gap with simulator-grounded scenes, structured event graphs, and controlled changes to the scene. The broader question is simple: when does generation become modeling?

Looking forward

From prediction toward intervention, mechanism, and discovery.

I want to build AI systems that are useful because they address the scientific bottleneck that matters. The work begins by deciding which question, representation, and evidence can carry a meaningful claim.

In biology, I am interested in moving from prediction toward perturbation, intervention, and mechanism—models that can help us reason about what will change, why it changes, and how it might be deliberately altered.

In generative world models, I am interested in moving from plausibility toward structured dynamics and causality—models whose predictions remain coherent under intervention and whose internal understanding can be tested rather than assumed.

AI is becoming increasingly capable of optimizing the objectives we specify. Science still requires us to decide which objectives are worth optimizing.
